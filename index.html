About the association

Who we are

The Wild Goose Lake Protection Association (WGLPA) is a nonprofit organization of property owners and friends of Wild Goose Lake in Lyndon Township, Washtenaw County, about five miles north of Chelsea, Michigan, next to Waterloo State Recreation Area. About number households belong today. Our application for 501(c)(3) tax-exempt status is pending with the IRS.

Membership is open to lakefront owners / anyone who loves the lake: confirm. Dues are $ amount per year and go straight to lake monitoring, aquatic plant management and community events.

Mission

To protect and improve the ecological health, recreational value and natural beauty of Wild Goose Lake for the people and wildlife who depend on it today and for generations to come.

Our goal is to catch problems early. We test the water, track changes from year to year, and act on what we learn, whether that means managing invasive plants, educating boaters or working with local and state agencies.

Lake facts

Our history

A lake community since 1927

Wild Goose was founded by Black families who wanted a lake of their own, at a time when many resorts nearby turned them away.

In 1927, three Black families from Ann Arbor, the Bakers, the Grayers and the Popes, bought the 250-acre Schultz farm around Wild Goose Lake. The sale closed on June 1, 1927. Charles Baker, co-owner of the Ann Arbor Foundry, organized the purchase with Donald Grayer and Iva Pope, and together they founded the Wild Goose Country Club.

Not every neighbor welcomed them. Some circulated a petition urging the owners not to sell, but the sale went through, and local grocer Perry Noah refused to sign it.

The club laid out 93 cottage lots, a shared stretch of shoreline with a fishing dock, a nine-hole golf course and a dance hall on the hill. The old farmhouse became the clubhouse. The first cottages were log cabins built from Sears, Roebuck kits, and lots sold for $100.

The Depression slowed building, a storm in the 1930s knocked down the dance hall, and the golf course has long since grown over. Old apple, pear and cherry trees from the farm's orchard outlasted them all, and a descendant of the Baker family still lives on the lake today.

Sources: Grace Shackman, “A Tale of Two Lakes,” Community Observer, Fall 2004; WEMU, February 2026.

Fishing

Fishing Wild Goose Lake

Fishing has been part of life here since the club built its dock on the shared shoreline in the 1920s. Much of the lake is shallow, with a deep hole of about 18 feet toward the east end.

The state has never stocked or surveyed Wild Goose Lake, so there's no official species list yet. Tell us what you catch and we'll keep a record.

What anglers report: largemouth bass and black crappie.

Getting on the water: There's no public boat launch, and the shoreline is almost all private. Please fish from your own property or as a guest of a resident. The nearest public launch is on North Lake.

Licenses: Anyone 17 or older needs a Michigan fishing license. In 2026 a resident annual license is $26, or $11 for residents 65 and older.

Statewide rules (2026): Bass are catch-and-release until the Saturday before Memorial Day, then may be kept through December 31 at 14 inches or longer, up to 5 a day. Bluegill, crappie and other sunfish are open all year with no minimum size and a limit of 25 a day combined. Northern pike may be kept from the last Saturday in April through March 15 at 24 inches or longer, 2 a day.

Rules change each April. Check the Michigan DNR fishing regulations before you go.

Our neighbors

Sandhill cranes

Sandhill cranes nest and feed around Wild Goose Lake, and their rolling, trumpeting calls are part of life on the lake. Here are a few things worth knowing about our long-legged neighbors.

4 ft

Tall, gray and red-capped

A sandhill crane stands about 4 feet tall with a wingspan of about 6.5 feet. The red cap is bare skin, not feathers, and it turns a deeper red when the bird is excited.

2.5 mi

Calls that carry

You can hear a crane up to 2.5 miles away. Its windpipe is so long that it coils inside the breastbone like a trumpet, which deepens the call and adds rich overtones.

20+ yrs

Partners for life

Cranes mate for life, often for 20 years or more, and pairs stay together all year. Mated pairs sing a duet called the unison call, with the female answering two notes for each of the male's.

Bow & leap

They dance

Cranes bow, pump their heads, spread their wings and spring into the air. Males and females both dance, to win a mate and to strengthen a bond they already have.

Rust

Mud makeup

In spring, cranes rub iron-rich mud into their gray feathers with their bills, staining them rusty brown. No one knows for sure why, but the leading idea is camouflage on the nest.

8 hrs

Colts on the move

Baby cranes are called colts. Within 8 hours of hatching they can leave the nest and even swim after their parents. They fly at about 10 weeks and stay with the family for 9 to 10 months.

2.5 million

An ancient bird

The oldest known sandhill crane fossil, found in Florida, is about 2.5 million years old. The oldest wild crane on record lived at least 37 years.

27 pairs

A Michigan comeback

Lost wetlands and market hunting left only 27 pairs in the Lower Peninsula by 1944. Under federal protection they rebounded to an estimated 24,000 cranes statewide by 2013.

Where to see thousands

Each fall, cranes gather near Waterloo to rest and feed before flying south. The best place to watch is Michigan Audubon's Haehnle Sanctuary, about 1,000 acres on the western edge of Waterloo Recreation Area in Jackson County. In the late afternoon, flocks drop into Mud Lake Marsh to roost. Late October through November is usually the peak. The sanctuary is free and open dawn to dusk.

Counters there tallied a record 8,177 cranes on November 19, 2012. Some cranes stay for the winter, too: the 2023–24 Waterloo Christmas Bird Count found 3,507, the most of any count in Michigan that year.

Living alongside cranes

Never feed them. Cranes that get handouts learn to raid bird feeders and peck at windows, and it brings them close to dogs, raccoons and foxes. If cranes keep visiting your feeder, take it down for a few days.

Give families room. Parents walking with colts often won't fly away, so slow down and let them cross roads and lawns at their own pace.

They're protected. Sandhill cranes are covered by federal migratory bird law, and Michigan has no crane hunting season.

Sources: Cornell Lab of Ornithology, All About Birds; Michigan DNR, sandhill crane viewing and its 2016 Migratory Bird Treaty centennial bulletin; Michigan Audubon; Jackson Audubon Society; U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service; National Audubon Society Christmas Bird Count.

Hunting

Waterfowl hunting near the lake

Glaciers left the country around Wild Goose Lake dotted with kettle lakes, marshes and farm fields, and the state land at Waterloo and Pinckney alone holds dozens of lakes, ponds and wetlands. That mix of open water, cover and food draws ducks and geese, and Michigan sits in the Mississippi Flyway, one of North America's four waterfowl flyways.

Canada geese nest in every Michigan county and are most common in the southern third of the state, so the DNR adds early September and late-winter goose seasons aimed at these year-round residents.

On Wild Goose Lake itself: The shoreline is almost all private, and Michigan law bars hunting with a firearm within 450 feet of an occupied home or farm building without the owner's or occupant's written permission. The law makes no exception for hunting from the water.

Goose Lake State Game Area

About 202 acres of marsh, open water, fields and woods surrounding Goose Lake in Sylvan Township, southwest of Chelsea. It's a different lake from ours, about 11 miles away as the crow flies. The DNR manages it first of all to raise waterfowl, with mallards and wood ducks among its featured species, and waterfowl and deer hunters use it most. Parking and a half-mile walking trail are on Rank Road, about 4 miles south of the Kalmbach Road exit on I-94. There's no boat launch.

DNR area map · DNR management plan

Other public land close by

Waterloo Recreation Area, which reaches into Lyndon Township, and Pinckney Recreation Area allow hunting in most places outside posted safety zones, though parts of Waterloo are closed to waterfowl hunting Sept. 1–15. A Recreation Passport is required for vehicles. Unadilla State Wildlife Area (about 1,160 acres of marsh and lakes, northwest of us), Chelsea State Game Area (about 800 acres, east of Chelsea) and Gregory State Game Area (about 2,700 acres, to the north) are also open to hunting. The DNR's Mi-HUNT map shows exact boundaries.

What you'll need

Licenses: A Michigan base license, a Michigan waterfowl license (which includes federal HIP registration) and, at 16 or older, a federal duck stamp. Anyone born on or after January 1, 1960 needs a hunter education certificate, or can start on an apprentice license with a licensed mentor.

Gear: Non-toxic shot only, such as steel. Hunter orange isn't required for waterfowl.

Boats: Shut off the motor and let the boat stop before you shoot, and keep guns unloaded while under power.

Private land: Always get the landowner's permission first.

Seasons and limits change every year. Check the DNR's 2026 waterfowl regulations before you go.

On the water

Boating safety and courtesy

A busy summer weekend works best when everyone follows the same rules. Local ordinances take priority; check them before you launch.

Slow-no-wake hours: e.g. sunset to 10 a.m.

Travel direction: Most lakes run counter-clockwise. confirm for Wild Goose Lake

Clean, drain, dry: Remove plants and mud, drain all water, and let your boat dry before moving between lakes. This is the single best way to stop invasive species.

Life jackets: Keep one for every person aboard, and make sure kids wear theirs.

Lake studies

What we measure, and why

Good decisions start with good data. These programs track the lake's condition year to year.

Water quality monitoring

Volunteers measure water clarity with a Secchi disk, along with temperature and dissolved oxygen, through the open-water season. Name the state volunteer monitoring program you take part in, if any.

Phosphorus and chlorophyll samples, when taken, show how much algae the lake can support.

Aquatic plants and invasive species

Native plants shelter fish and hold the lake bottom in place. Invasive species such as Eurasian watermilfoil and starry stonewort can crowd them out. We survey the lake and coordinate any treatment with state permits.
